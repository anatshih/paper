VALIDITY ANALYSIS REPORT

Stage 6: Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) and Construct Validity

Study Title:

The Mediating Role of Job Satisfaction in the Relationship Between

Psychological Capital and Work Engagement Among Academic Staff

at Al-Quds Open University, Palestine

Sample Size: N = 270 Academic Staff Members

Statistical Software: IBM SPSS Statistics v26

Date: May 2026

1. Introduction

Validity analysis constitutes a critical pillar of quantitative research quality, complementing the reliability assessment conducted in Stage 5. While reliability confirms that a measurement instrument produces consistent scores, validity addresses the more fundamental question of whether the instrument actually measures the construct it claims to measure. In the context of this study, construct validity was assessed through Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) — a multivariate statistical technique that examines the underlying factor structure of each measurement instrument and evaluates the extent to which observed items cluster onto theoretically meaningful dimensions (Hair et al., 2019; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019).

The study investigates the mediating role of Job Satisfaction (measured by the MSQ-20) in the relationship between Psychological Capital (measured by the PCQ-24) and Work Engagement (measured by the UWES-9) among academic staff at Al-Quds Open University (QOU), Palestine. For the hypothesized relationships between these constructs to be meaningfully tested, each instrument must demonstrate adequate construct validity — confirming that the items within each scale genuinely reflect the theoretical dimensions they purport to capture. This is especially important in a Palestinian higher education context, where internationally validated instruments are being applied to a population with distinct cultural, institutional, and socio-political characteristics.

Exploratory factor analysis procedures were conducted in SPSS using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) as the extraction method, with Varimax rotation (where applicable) in IBM SPSS Statistics. Prior to factor extraction, two statistical prerequisites were examined: the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) Measure of Sampling Adequacy and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity. Together, these tests confirm whether the correlation structure of the data is suitable for factor analysis (Kaiser, 1974; Field, 2018).

2. Analytical Criteria and Decision Rules

The following criteria were applied consistently across all three factor analyses, in line with established best practice guidelines (Hair et al., 2019; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019; Costello & Osborne, 2005):

KMO Measure of Sampling Adequacy: Values above .80 are considered meritorious, and values above .90 are classified as marvelous (Kaiser, 1974). A KMO value below .50 would render factor analysis inadvisable.

Bartlett's Test of Sphericity: Statistical significance (p < .05) is required to confirm that the inter-item correlation matrix is not an identity matrix and that the variables share sufficient common variance for factor extraction (Field, 2018).

Factor Extraction: Factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 (Kaiser's criterion) were retained as the primary extraction guide. The number of theoretically expected factors was also specified a priori based on the instrument's published structure.

Factor Loadings: A minimum loading of .40 was adopted as the threshold for an item to be considered as loading meaningfully on a factor, consistent with the guideline that loadings of .40 or above with a sample of N ≥ 200 are practically significant (Hair et al., 2019). Items loading above .50 are considered strong contributors to their respective factors.

Total Variance Explained: A minimum of 50% cumulative variance explained across retained factors was used as a benchmark for acceptable construct coverage (Hair et al., 2019).

The particularly high KMO values observed in this Palestinian academic sample are noteworthy. They suggest that QOU faculty members respond to items within each scale in a coherent and patterned manner, which may reflect the shared occupational experiences, cultural values, and institutional context that academic staff at QOU commonly navigate. This degree of response coherence supports the view that internationally validated constructs — PsyCap, work engagement, and job satisfaction — are not merely imported conceptual abstractions but are psychologically meaningful and experientially salient for this population.

The four-factor solution accounted for a cumulative 59.32% of the total variance in the PCQ-24 items, which is well above the 50% threshold recommended for adequate construct coverage (Hair et al., 2019). Prior to rotation, the first factor carried a dominant eigenvalue of 10.024, accounting for 41.77% of variance — a pattern characteristic of higher-order constructs where a general factor accounts for substantial shared variance across all items. This is entirely consistent with the theoretical conceptualization of PsyCap as a second-order construct comprising four interrelated but distinguishable positive psychological resources (Luthans et al., 2007; Avey et al., 2011).

After Varimax rotation, the variance was more evenly distributed across the four factors: Factor 1 = 15.28%, Factor 2 = 14.89%, Factor 3 = 14.87%, and Factor 4 = 14.28%. This near-equal distribution of variance following rotation is a positive indicator, suggesting that the four dimensions of PsyCap are relatively balanced in their contributions to the overall construct — none dominates at the expense of the others. This balance is theoretically meaningful, as Luthans et al. (2007) conceptualize the four PsyCap dimensions as complementary and mutually reinforcing components of a unified psychological resource pool.

The rotated factor solution reveals a clean and theoretically interpretable four-factor structure for the PCQ-24. All 24 items loaded on their primary factors with loadings ranging from .563 to .753, all exceeding the .40 minimum threshold and the majority (.563 to .753) classified as strong loadings above .50. Importantly, every item loaded most strongly on its theoretically designated dimension, confirming that the PCQ-24 captures the four PsyCap dimensions as distinct yet interrelated constructs among QOU faculty.

Factor 1 captured the six Self-Efficacy items (loadings: .624–.716), reflecting confidence in performing academic tasks and representing one's work area. The highest loading on this factor was observed for pcq6 ("I feel confident presenting information to a group of colleagues"; loading = .716), which makes intuitive sense in an academic context where faculty members regularly engage in classroom delivery, conference presentations, and committee participation.

Factor 2 captured the six Resilience items (loadings: .563–.726), reflecting the capacity to manage difficulties, sustain performance under stress, and recover from setbacks. The relatively strong loadings on this factor may suggest that Resilience is a psychologically salient dimension for this sample. The emergence of Resilience as a clearly defined factor underscores its salience as a psychologically distinct and experientially meaningful dimension of PsyCap in this context.

Factor 3 captured the six Hope items (loadings: .644–.716), reflecting goal-directed thinking, agency, and the generation of alternative pathways. Factor 4 captured the six Optimism items (loadings: .600–.753), reflecting positive expectancies about future work outcomes. The cross-loadings observed across factors are modest and expected for a higher-order construct where dimensions share conceptual overlap — they do not compromise the interpretability of the four-factor solution.

Overall, the PCQ-24 factor structure obtained in this Palestinian academic sample broadly aligns with the four-factor structure reported in the instrument’s original validation (Luthans et al., 2007) and in subsequent cross-cultural replications (Avey et al., 2011), providing adequate preliminary evidence for the construct validity of the PCQ-24 as a measure of psychological capital at QOU.

5. Exploratory Factor Analysis — UWES-9 (Work Engagement)

5.1 Factor Structure and Variance Explained

For the UWES-9, a one-factor solution was specified consistent with Schaufeli et al.'s (2006) recommendation that the nine items be used as a unidimensional composite measure of overall work engagement, particularly in research where engagement is treated as a single higher-order latent variable. PCA without rotation was conducted. The single extracted factor yielded an eigenvalue of 4.791, explaining 53.24% of the total variance — comfortably exceeding the 50% benchmark. The second factor's eigenvalue of .846 (below 1.0) confirmed the adequacy of the one-factor solution.

All nine UWES items demonstrated strong loadings on the single factor, ranging from .655 to .760, with every loading exceeding .60. This uniformly high loading pattern supports the interpretation that the nine items are drawing on a single, coherent underlying construct — work engagement — and that this construct is psychometrically robust in the QOU faculty sample.

Across the three subscales, the loading pattern reflects subtle but theoretically interesting differences. The Dedication items (uwes4: .746; uwes5: .752; uwes6: .739) and Absorption items (uwes8: .742; uwes9: .760) produced the highest loadings, suggesting that the cognitive immersion and meaning-oriented aspects of engagement are particularly salient dimensions for QOU academic staff. The Vigor items — especially uwes3 ("When I get up in the morning, I feel like going to work"; loading = .655, communality = .429) — showed relatively lower loadings, which is consistent with the finding from Stage 5 that morning motivation scores were slightly lower among the sample.

This pattern suggests that for Palestinian academic staff, work engagement is experienced and expressed most powerfully through dedication and absorption — a deep sense of meaning, enthusiasm, and immersive focus — rather than through sheer energetic vigor. This may reflect the nature of academic work itself, which demands sustained intellectual engagement and intrinsic motivation, and which tends to activate identity-based forms of engagement even when energy levels fluctuate in response to external stressors.

The one-factor solution for UWES-9 supports the use of a composite total score for work engagement in all subsequent regression and mediation analyses, consistent with the instrument's scoring guidelines (Schaufeli et al., 2006).

6. Exploratory Factor Analysis — MSQ-20 (Job Satisfaction)

6.1 Factor Structure and Variance Explained

The MSQ-20 was analyzed using PCA with Varimax rotation, specifying a two-factor solution aligned with the instrument's theoretical distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic job satisfaction facets (Weiss et al., 1967; Spector, 1997). Table 5 presents the variance explained before and after rotation.

The two-factor solution explained 50.81% of the total variance in the MSQ-20 items, meeting the 50% threshold for adequate construct coverage. Prior to rotation, a dominant first factor (eigenvalue = 9.298, 46.49% of variance) emerged — a pattern consistent with a general satisfaction factor underlying all 20 facets. After Varimax rotation, the variance was redistributed between Factor 1 (28.18%) and Factor 2 (22.63%), indicating a more balanced two-factor structure in which intrinsic and extrinsic satisfaction facets are distinguishable but related dimensions of the overall satisfaction construct.

The strong first eigenvalue (9.298) prior to rotation, combined with the relatively modest separation between the two post-rotation factors, is typical of MSQ-20 analyses in educational settings and does not undermine the interpretability of the two-factor solution. Rather, it reflects the empirical reality that intrinsic and extrinsic job satisfaction, while conceptually distinct, share considerable common variance because faculty members who are satisfied with one aspect of their job tend to be satisfied with others — a halo effect that is well-documented in the job satisfaction literature (Spector, 1997).

The rotated factor solution yields a theoretically meaningful and interpretable two-factor structure for the MSQ-20. Factor 1 (Intrinsic Satisfaction) captured items related to the nature of the work itself — including variety, community recognition, moral congruence, autonomy, pay, advancement, working conditions, and supervisory quality. Factor 2 (Extrinsic/Social Satisfaction) captured items related to social and relational aspects of work — including opportunities for social influence, altruistic service, collegial relationships, recognition, accomplishment, and work method autonomy.

It is worth noting that the two-factor structure emerging from this Palestinian sample does not map perfectly onto the classical intrinsic-extrinsic distinction proposed by Weiss et al. (1967). Several items — including pay, advancement, and supervision — loaded more strongly on Factor 1 than the classical model would predict, while items reflecting social relationships and recognition loaded on Factor 2. This pattern may reflect the specific value system and social orientation of Palestinian academic staff, for whom institutional and structural factors (pay, policies, advancement) and relational and recognition factors (collegial relations, praise, social contribution) represent distinct experiential dimensions of satisfaction. This nuance is worth acknowledging in the study's discussion.

The highest-loading item on Factor 2 was "The chance to try my own methods of doing the job" (loading = .784), followed by "The chance to tell people what to do" (.707) and "The feeling of accomplishment I get from the job" (.628). This pattern suggests that among QOU faculty, professional autonomy, influence, and a sense of accomplishment are the relatively stronger indicators of the second satisfaction dimension — consistent with the broader academic identity literature, where intrinsic rewards of intellectual freedom and impact constitute core sources of work meaning.

It should also be noted that several MSQ-20 items showed relatively close loadings across the two factors — for example, “The chance to do something that uses my abilities” (.478 vs. .498) and “The chance to do things for other people” (.497 vs. .499). These borderline cross-loadings indicate some overlap between intrinsic and extrinsic/social satisfaction facets. Accordingly, the two-factor solution should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as an exploratory structure.

Despite the nuanced factor pattern, all 20 MSQ items loaded above the .40 threshold on at least one factor, suggesting that no items are redundant or poorly fitting within the two-factor structure. This supports the use of a composite total satisfaction score (combining all 20 items) in the mediation analysis, while acknowledging that the two dimensions may contribute differentially to the mediation pathway — a point that warrants discussion in Stage 9.

The EFA results across all three scales are consistently strong and theoretically coherent. The PCQ-24 demonstrated a clear four-factor structure corresponding to Self-Efficacy, Resilience, Hope, and Optimism, with all 24 items loading on their theoretically designated dimensions. The UWES-9 confirmed a unidimensional structure of work engagement with all nine items producing strong loadings above .65. The MSQ-20 yielded a two-factor structure distinguishing between intrinsic and social satisfaction dimensions, with all 20 items loading meaningfully.

The KMO values for all three scales fall in the ‘marvelous’ range (≥ .90), and total variance explained ranges from 50.81% to 59.32% — all meeting or exceeding the recommended thresholds. Collectively, these findings provide acceptable preliminary evidence of the construct validity of all three measurement instruments in the context of Palestinian higher education at Al-Quds Open University.

These validity findings, combined with the excellent reliability coefficients established in Stage 5, provide a strong psychometric foundation for all subsequent analyses. The composite scores used in the correlation analysis (Stage 7), regression models (Stage 8), and mediation testing (Stage 9) are supported by both the internal consistency and the factor-analytic validity of the three instruments, thereby maximising the accuracy and interpretability of the study's main findings.
