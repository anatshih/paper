1. Purpose and Rationale

Prior to conducting inferential statistical analyses — including Pearson correlation, multiple regression, and PROCESS-based mediation analysis — it is methodologically necessary to verify that the collected data satisfy the foundational statistical assumptions upon which these procedures depend. Violations of these assumptions may compromise the validity and interpretability of the results, potentially leading to inflated Type I error rates, biased regression coefficient estimates, or reduced statistical power (Field, 2018; Hair et al., 2019; Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019). Stage 4 therefore undertakes a systematic and comprehensive examination of the key statistical assumptions governing the planned analyses in this study.

The present report examines four categories of assumptions: (1) normality of the distribution of the three composite study variables, assessed through both quantitative indices and visual inspection; (2) homogeneity of variance across demographic groups, assessed using Levene's Test of Equality of Variances; (3) absence of problematic multicollinearity among the predictor and mediator variables, assessed through the Pearson correlation matrix; and (4) absence of influential outliers that could distort the findings. The assumption of independence of observations is also addressed and confirmed on the basis of the study's research design. These assumptions collectively underpin the inferential analyses to be conducted in Stages 7 through 9 of the research framework.

All three composite variables are examined: Psychological Capital (PCQ-24 mean score, pcq_total_mean), Work Engagement (UWES-9 mean score, uwes_total_mean), and Job Satisfaction (MSQ-20 mean score, msq_total_mean). The SPSS EXAMINE procedure was used to generate descriptive statistics, formal normality tests, Q-Q plots, and boxplots. Levene's Test was conducted via the SPSS ONEWAY procedure. The Pearson correlation matrix was generated via the SPSS CORRELATIONS procedure. All output was produced on 17 May 2026 using IBM SPSS Statistics v26 and is documented in Assumption_Testing.pdf.

3. Normality Assessment

3.1 Skewness and Kurtosis Indices

Normality was first assessed by examining the skewness and kurtosis values of each composite variable. These statistics provide direct quantitative information about the shape of the distribution: skewness quantifies asymmetry, while kurtosis quantifies the relative heaviness of the distribution's tails. Values of skewness and kurtosis within the range of ±1.0 are generally considered indicative of acceptable normality for parametric statistical analysis in applied social research (Kim, 2013; Hair et al., 2019). Table 2 presents these indices alongside their standard errors and an overall normality assessment.

All three skewness values fall well within the acceptable range of ±1.0. The PCQ Total Mean exhibits minimal negative skewness (−.359), while the UWES and MSQ display comparable slight negative skewness (−.371 and −.396, respectively), indicating that respondents across all three measures tend toward moderate-to-high response values. This pattern is characteristic of self-report Likert-scale instruments administered to employed academic samples and is consistent with findings in the broader occupational psychology literature (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019). Kurtosis values are similarly acceptable for all three variables (−.283, −.252, and −.312), indicating platykurtic but near-normal distributions without excessive peakedness or heavy tails.

The slight negative skewness observed across all three variables carries substantive significance in the QOU context. Faculty members responding to the PCQ-24, UWES-9, and MSQ-20 may reflect professional identity and institutional commitment characteristic of academic staff, even under challenging working conditions — including salary constraints, workload pressures, and the broader political and socio-economic environment in Palestine. This positive self-assessment tendency is consistent with the literature on professional identity resilience in academic settings (Luthans et al., 2007).

3.2 Formal Normality Tests: Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk

In addition to skewness and kurtosis inspection, formal normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test with Lilliefors correction and the Shapiro-Wilk (S-W) test, both generated via the SPSS EXAMINE procedure. The Shapiro-Wilk test is generally preferred for sample sizes up to N = 2,000 owing to its superior statistical power for detecting departures from normality (Razali & Wah, 2011; Shapiro & Wilk, 1965). Table 3 presents the results.

The formal normality tests yielded statistically significant results for all three variables: PCQ Total Mean (K-S = .055, p = .046; S-W = .984, p = .003), UWES Total Mean (K-S = .080, p < .001; S-W = .984, p = .004), and MSQ Total Mean (K-S = .067, p = .005; S-W = .980, p = .001). However, it is methodologically established that formal normality tests are hypersensitive to minor distributional deviations as sample size increases (Field, 2018; Pallant, 2020). With N = 270, even trivially small departures from theoretical normality will produce significant test results. The Shapiro-Wilk statistics (.980–.984) are all very close to 1.0, indicating only negligible departures from normality.

Furthermore, the Central Limit Theorem provides robust theoretical justification for parametric methods at this sample size. For samples exceeding n = 30, the sampling distribution of the mean converges to normality regardless of the population distribution; at N = 270, this convergence is virtually guaranteed (Field, 2018; Hair et al., 2019). Combined with the acceptable skewness and kurtosis indices presented in Table 2, the overall evidence supports the conclusion that the distributional properties of all three composite variables are sufficiently normal for valid parametric inference.

3.3 Visual Inspection: Histograms, Q-Q Plots, and Boxplots

Visual inspection of the histograms, Normal Q-Q plots, Detrended Normal Q-Q plots, and boxplots generated by the SPSS EXAMINE procedure (Assumption_Testing.pdf, pp. 5–10) provided additional confirmation of the quantitative findings.

The histogram for PCQ Total Mean (Mean = 4.13, SD = .639) displays an approximately unimodal, bell-shaped distribution with a minor negative skew. The histogram for UWES Total Mean (Mean = 4.23, SD = .909) shows a roughly symmetrical distribution, with slight multimodality at the 3.5 and 4.5 regions consistent with the discrete character of the 7-point frequency scale. The histogram for MSQ Total Mean (Mean = 3.69, SD = .691) is the most symmetric of the three, displaying a clearly bell-shaped, near-Gaussian distribution.

In all three Normal Q-Q plots, the observed data points align closely with the diagonal reference line across the central range of each variable, with only minor deviations at the lower and upper tails — consistent with the mild skewness quantified in Table 2. The Detrended Q-Q plots further confirm that distributional deviations from zero are small, random, and unsystematic, without any curved or patterned structure that would indicate a substantively non-normal distribution. The boxplots identified only isolated mild outliers at the lower tail (Cases 139, 210, and 241), which are examined in detail in Section 6.

4. Homogeneity of Variance — Levene's Test

Homogeneity of variance — the assumption that the population variances of the dependent variables are equal across comparison groups — was assessed using Levene's Test of Equality of Variances (based on mean), with gender as the grouping variable (1 = Male, n = 173; 2 = Female, n = 97). This assumption is relevant to independent-samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA procedures planned for Stages 7 and 8, and provides general confirmation that the variance structure of the data is stable across the primary demographic grouping variable (Field, 2018). Levene's Test was non-significant for all three composite variables: PCQ Total Mean (F(1, 268) = 1.216, p = .271), UWES Total Mean (F(1, 268) = .022, p = .881), and MSQ Total Mean (F(1, 268) = 1.103, p = .294). All p-values substantially exceed the α = .05 criterion, confirming that the assumption of homogeneity of variance is satisfied across gender groups for all three study variables.

The near-zero Levene statistic for Work Engagement (F = .022, p = .881) is particularly notable, indicating virtually identical variance between male and female faculty members on this measure — suggesting that the dispersion of work engagement scores is highly uniform across gender within the QOU faculty population. This result confirms that equal-variance parametric tests are appropriate for all planned gender-group comparisons, without the need for Welch's correction. Additionally, the homogeneity finding supports the stability and generalisability of the regression coefficients to be estimated in Stage 8 across both gender groups.

5. Multicollinearity Assessment

The absence of problematic multicollinearity — a condition in which independent or mediating variables are so strongly intercorrelated that they cannot be distinguished analytically — was assessed by examining the Pearson bivariate correlation matrix among the three study variables. The commonly adopted threshold for indicating potentially problematic multicollinearity is r ≥ .80 (Hair et al., 2019; Field, 2018); correlations substantially below this threshold support the discriminant validity of the variables and the interpretability of regression coefficients. All bivariate correlations are positive and statistically significant at p < .001 (two-tailed). The strongest association is observed between Work Engagement and Job Satisfaction (r = .651), followed by Psychological Capital and Work Engagement (r = .529), and Psychological Capital and Job Satisfaction (r = .482). The pattern of correlations is theoretically congruent with the study's conceptual model: Psychological Capital is positively associated with both Work Engagement (the dependent variable) and Job Satisfaction (the proposed mediator), and Job Satisfaction is positively associated with Work Engagement. These relationships are consistent with the Conservation of Resources Theory (Hobfoll, 1989) and the Positive Organisational Behaviour framework (Luthans et al., 2007), which both predict that positive psychological resources will correlate positively with motivational and attitudinal work outcomes. The inter-predictor correlation (PCQ–MSQ: r = .482) further supports the plausibility of a mediated pathway and is well within acceptable bounds for mediation analysis (Hayes, 2018).

These cases represent authentic individual variation and are retained in the dataset, as they do not constitute statistically influential leverage points that would distort the regression estimates or invalidate the inferential findings.

7. Independence of Observations

The assumption of independence of observations requires that each participant's responses are not influenced by, and do not influence, the responses of any other participant. This assumption is satisfied by the study's cross-sectional survey design, in which each of the 270 QOU academic staff members completed the survey instrument independently, without knowledge of or interaction with other respondents' answers. No repeated measures, cluster sampling with shared environments, or time-series components are present in the data structure. Respondents were drawn from 15 geographically distributed branches across the West Bank and Gaza Strip, and the survey was administered under conditions that precluded response sharing. Accordingly, the independence assumption is considered met by design and does not require formal statistical verification (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019; Field, 2018).

9. Conclusion and Implications for Subsequent Analyses

The comprehensive assumption testing conducted in Stage 4 confirms that the dataset is fully suitable for the planned parametric inferential analyses. The key findings are summarised as follows. First, the normality assumption is practically satisfied: although formal K-S and Shapiro-Wilk tests yielded statistically significant results (as is expected given N = 270), the Shapiro-Wilk statistics (.980–.984) indicate only negligible departures from normality, and the skewness and kurtosis indices for all three variables fall well within the ±1.0 criterion. Visual inspection confirmed approximately bell-shaped distributions with close adherence to the diagonal in Normal Q-Q plots. The Central Limit Theorem provides additional theoretical justification for parametric methods at this sample size.

Second, the homogeneity of variance assumption is confirmed: Levene's Test was non-significant for all three variables across gender groups (p = .271 to .881), indicating equal variances that support the use of standard equal-variance parametric tests. Third, the absence of problematic multicollinearity is established: all bivariate correlations among the study variables fall within the range r = .482–.651, well below the .80 threshold, confirming the analytical separability of the three constructs. Fourth, the identified extreme values are substantively interpretable within the Palestinian academic context and do not constitute influential outliers requiring removal. Fifth, independence of observations is satisfied by design.

Collectively, these findings confirm that the data meet all necessary statistical prerequisites for Pearson correlation analysis (Stage 7), multiple regression analysis (Stage 8), and PROCESS-based mediation analysis (Stage 9). The study proceeds to Stage 5: Reliability Analysis with full confidence in the methodological integrity of the dataset.
