1. Introduction

Reliability analysis is a foundational prerequisite in any quantitative study employing psychometric scales. Before inferential conclusions about inter-construct relationships can be drawn with confidence, it is essential to establish that each measurement instrument demonstrates adequate internal consistency — that is, that the items constituting each scale converge on a single, coherent underlying construct in a stable and replicable manner. In the absence of sufficient reliability, observed associations among variables may reflect random measurement error rather than genuine construct relationships, thereby undermining the validity of any subsequent inferential conclusions (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994).

This report presents a comprehensive reliability analysis of all three measurement instruments employed in the present study, which examines the mediating role of Job Satisfaction in the relationship between Psychological Capital and Work Engagement among academic staff members at Al-Quds Open University (QOU), Palestine. Given the unique socio-political, institutional, and cultural context in which this study is conducted, it is particularly important to verify that internationally validated instruments demonstrate adequate reliability when applied to this specific population (Avey et al., 2011; Luthans et al., 2007).

The three instruments assessed are: (1) the Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ-24; Luthans et al., 2007), measuring the independent variable; (2) the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale — short form (UWES-9; Schaufeli et al., 2006), measuring the dependent variable; and (3) the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire short-form (MSQ-20; Weiss et al., 1967), measuring the mediating variable. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach's alpha coefficient (α), the most widely adopted index of internal consistency in social and organisational science research (Cronbach, 1951; Field, 2018). Item-level diagnostics, including Corrected Item-Total Correlations (CITC) and Cronbach's Alpha if Item Deleted statistics, were also examined to evaluate the contribution of each item to the reliability of its respective scale.

2. Reliability Criteria and Interpretive Benchmarks

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha (α), with interpretation guided by the classification framework proposed by George and Mallery (2020), which is among the most widely cited in applied behavioural research, and cross-referenced with the foundational standards established by Nunnally and Bernstein (1994). In addition to the overall alpha coefficient, item-level diagnostics were examined for each scale. The Corrected Item-Total Correlation (CITC) indicates the degree to which each item correlates with the total scale score, with its own contribution partialled out. Items with CITC values below .30 are generally considered weak contributors to a scale and may be candidates for removal (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). The Alpha if Item Deleted statistic indicates whether removing a given item would meaningfully improve the overall reliability coefficient; a substantial increase would suggest that the item does not fit well within the scale (Hair et al., 2019). All items were retained unless both criteria jointly indicated poor fit.

3. Reliability Analysis — Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ-24)

3.1 Instrument Overview

The PCQ-24 (Luthans et al., 2007) is a 24-item instrument measuring four theoretically distinct and empirically validated dimensions of psychological capital: Self-Efficacy (items 1–6; the belief in one's capacity to mobilise cognitive resources and execute tasks successfully in specific contexts), Hope (items 7–12; goal-directed thinking involving both the motivation to pursue goals and the capacity to generate alternative pathways), Resilience (items 13–18; the capacity to recover, adapt, and grow from adversity, uncertainty, and setbacks), and Optimism (items 19–24; a positive attributional style characterised by the expectation of positive outcomes). Each subscale contains six items rated on a six-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 6 (Strongly Agree). Three items (pcq13, pcq20, and pcq23) are negatively worded and were reverse-scored prior to analysis in accordance with the instrument's scoring protocol (denoted as pcq13_r, pcq20_r, pcq23_r).

In the present study, Psychological Capital constitutes the independent variable hypothesised to exert both direct and indirect effects on work engagement, with the indirect pathway operating through job satisfaction as the mediating mechanism. Strong psychometric reliability of the PCQ-24 is therefore a prerequisite for detecting these hypothesised effects with adequate statistical precision.

The PCQ-24 demonstrated excellent internal consistency in the present sample, yielding a Cronbach's alpha of α = .939 across all 24 items (N = 270). This value substantially exceeds the .90 threshold for excellent reliability (George & Mallery, 2020) and surpasses the minimum standard of .70 typically required for research purposes (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). The alpha coefficient based on standardised items was identical (α = .939), indicating that the reliability estimate is not an artefact of differences in item scaling or response format. These results strongly support the internal consistency of the PCQ-24 and justify the computation and use of a composite psychological capital score in all subsequent inferential analyses.

Examination of item-level statistics revealed that all 24 PCQ items demonstrated acceptable-to-strong corrected item-total correlations, ranging from CITC = .510 (pcq20_r) to CITC = .666 (pcq12: "At this time, I am meeting the work goals that I have set for myself"). Critically, every CITC value exceeds the recommended minimum threshold of .30, confirming that all items contribute meaningfully to the measurement of psychological capital in this sample (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994).

The lowest CITC was observed for pcq20_r (.510), a reverse-scored Optimism item. Slight attenuation in item-total correlation for reverse-scored items is not uncommon and may be attributed to minor response pattern inconsistencies introduced by negatively worded stems (Weijters et al., 2013). Nevertheless, the value remains comfortably above the acceptable threshold and does not warrant item removal. Furthermore, the Alpha if Item Deleted statistic confirms that removing pcq20_r would increase alpha only marginally to .938 — a negligible difference that provides no substantive justification for exclusion.

The Alpha if Item Deleted values for all 24 items ranged from .935 to .938, indicating that the overall reliability estimate is highly stable and not dependent on any single item. This uniformity confirms the structural integrity of the full 24-item scale. All items were retained for subsequent analyses.

4. Reliability Analysis — Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9)

4.1 Instrument Overview

The UWES-9 (Schaufeli et al., 2006) is a psychometrically validated nine-item measure of work engagement, comprising three theoretically grounded dimensions: Vigor (items 1–3; characterised by high levels of energy, mental resilience, and persistent effort at work), Dedication (items 4–6; encompassing a sense of significance, enthusiasm, inspiration, pride, and challenge in one's work), and Absorption (items 7–9; reflecting full concentration, deep engrossment, and difficulty detaching oneself from work activities). The scale employs a seven-point frequency format ranging from 0 (Never) to 6 (Always/Every Day). In the present study, work engagement serves as the dependent variable, positioned as the terminal outcome in the hypothesised mediation model. The reliability of the UWES-9 is therefore critical for the detection of both direct and indirect effects with adequate statistical power.

5. Reliability Analysis — Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire (MSQ-20)

5.1 Instrument Overview

The MSQ-20 (Weiss et al., 1967) is a widely validated 20-item measure of overall job satisfaction, assessing satisfaction across twenty distinct facets of work that span both intrinsic dimensions (e.g., achievement, autonomy, variety, creativity, responsibility, recognition) and extrinsic dimensions (e.g., supervision quality, remuneration, working conditions, institutional policy implementation, advancement opportunities). Respondents rate their satisfaction on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Very Dissatisfied) to 5 (Very Satisfied). In the present study, job satisfaction functions as the mediating variable, theoretically positioned as the psychological mechanism through which psychological capital exerts its influence on work engagement. Establishing the reliability of the MSQ-20 is therefore essential to ensuring that the hypothesised mediation pathway can be detected and estimated with measurement precision.

All 20 MSQ items demonstrated acceptable-to-strong corrected item-total correlations, ranging from CITC = .570 ("The chance to try my own methods of doing the job") to CITC = .698 ("The chance to be somebody in the community"). No item would have meaningfully improved the alpha coefficient if deleted — the Alpha if Item Deleted values were uniformly stable in the range of .935 to .937 — confirming that the full 20-item structure is well-suited to measuring job satisfaction in this sample population.

The pay satisfaction item ("My pay and the amount of work I do") produced the lowest item mean in the MSQ-20 (M = 3.14, SD = 1.06), with the advancement item ("The chances for advancement on this job", M = 3.20, SD = 1.12) also among the lowest-rated facets. These findings may reflect the broader institutional and economic context of academic employment at QOU. In contrast, intrinsic satisfaction items — particularly those related to co-worker relations (M = 4.07), conscience-congruent work (M = 4.01), and doing things for other people (M = 3.97) — produced consistently higher ratings, suggesting that faculty members derive greater satisfaction from the relational and moral dimensions of academic work. All items were retained, as all CITC values are psychometrically acceptable.

The reliability evidence presented across Sections 3 through 5 confirms that all three measurement instruments demonstrate psychometrically acceptable-to-excellent internal consistency in the present sample of N = 270 QOU academic staff members. Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranged from α = .757 (UWES Vigor subscale) to α = .939 (PCQ-24 Total and MSQ-20 Total), with all total-scale coefficients at or above the good reliability threshold of .80. All item-level CITC values exceeded the minimum criterion of .30, indicating that all items contribute meaningfully to their respective scales. The Alpha if Item Deleted statistics confirmed the structural stability of each scale, with no item deletion materially improving the overall reliability estimates.

These results collectively establish a strong psychometric foundation for the inferential analyses to be conducted in subsequent stages. The reliable composite scores for Psychological Capital, Job Satisfaction, and Work Engagement support the use of Pearson correlation analysis (Stage 7), multiple regression analysis (Stage 8), and PROCESS-based mediation analysis (Stage 9) with confidence in the measurement quality of the study instruments.

7. Implications for Subsequent Analyses

The reliability evidence presented in this report carries the following methodological implications for the subsequent stages of statistical analysis:

Validity Analysis (Stage 6): The acceptable-to-excellent reliability coefficients provide a favourable psychometric basis for proceeding to validity assessment. However, internal consistency reliability alone does not establish construct validity. Exploratory or confirmatory factor analysis remains necessary to examine the dimensional structure of each instrument and to assess whether the items load on the theoretically expected factors.

Correlation Analysis (Stage 7): The reliable composite scores support the use of Pearson correlation analysis to examine bivariate associations among Psychological Capital, Job Satisfaction, and Work Engagement. High reliability reduces measurement error attenuation in the correlation coefficients, resulting in more accurate effect size estimates.

Regression Analysis (Stage 8): The satisfactory reliability of all three scales reduces concerns related to random measurement error in regression models. Composite scores may be used as predictor, mediator, and outcome variables with appropriate confidence in their psychometric adequacy.

Mediation Analysis (Stage 9): The strong internal consistency of all three constructs provides the measurement foundation for testing the proposed mediation model (Psychological Capital → Job Satisfaction → Work Engagement) using the PROCESS Macro (Hayes, 2018). However, the strength, direction, and statistical significance of the direct and indirect effects should be determined empirically through the mediation analysis, and should not be inferred from reliability evidence alone.
